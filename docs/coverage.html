What Bizzing English teaches — seven strands, ten levels, every one filled

Every strand of school English from 6 to 14 — words, sentences, reading, writing, speaking, literature and the language itself — checked against the app as it is built today: 7 strands × 10 levels, 70 of 70 levels with stops, 214 stops, of which 87 are passages from the classics and 24 are chapters of 2 whole books; 115 works in the Library (80 held in full and cleared in the US, UK and India, 32 as cards); seven games with memory, the Elocution Contest, a Tools shelf with Bee’s Vocabulary, Idioms, Typing Trainer and quotes, Greek myths and 14 author deep dives, My Feed of 5,996 cards, and a writing desk judged only by a grown-up. “Where” names the strand, level and stops that teach it, or the place in the app.

Three journeys

Where each age begins

The app never asks a child's age, only an age band. The band picks where each strand starts and which strands are open on day one; the rest open as the child goes.

  1. 6–7ages

    Starts at level 1 of every open strand

    Rhymes and word families, naming and doing words, capitals and full stops, fables and poems heard in the narrator’s voice, copywork, and reading aloud.

    WordSentenceReading

    Opens on the way: Writing (after Sentence 1), Speaking (after Reading 1), Literature (after Reading 2), Language (after Word 3).

    68 stops written for this age or younger.

  2. 8–10ages

    Starts at level 2 of every open strand

    Meanings, prefixes and suffixes, clauses and joining words, children’s classics and whole books, dictation and the paragraph, reciting by heart, story elements and borrowed words.

    WordSentenceReading

    Opens on the way: Writing (after Sentence 1), Speaking (after Reading 1), Literature (after Reading 2), Language (after Word 3).

    154 stops written for this age or younger.

  3. 11–14ages

    Starts at level 3 of every open strand

    Roots and their families, shades of meaning, parallel and periodic sentences, drama, essays and close reading, the essay and writing after a model, speeches and debate, literary periods, rhetoric and world Englishes.

Beyond the Atlas

The rooms around the road

The Library

87 passages told scene by scene over their paintings in the narrator's voice, each followed by seven exercises built from it. 115 works: 80 held in full, 32 cards (modern works are never quoted).

Whole books

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (12 chapters) and The Wind in the Willows (12 chapters) — a chapter a night, with a bookmark, the story so far, the people met so far and the chapters' notes for grown-ups.

Play

  • Sentence Builder — build sentences from real books, both orders, against the clock
  • Punctuation Rush — commas in 1,937 real sentences
  • Who Said It? — a detective: the line, then clues — guess early, score more
  • Figure Hunt — name the figure, then find it inside a passage
  • Plot Line — drag scenes onto a timeline
  • Root Forge — parts into real words; forge a family
  • Rhetoric Duel — the stronger line and why, best of five

Tools

The Stage and the Writing Desk, and from Bizzing Bee: Vocabulary (17 decks), Idioms & Similes (2,368 phrases), the Typing Trainer (15 lessons and a sixty-second test) and Quotes & Poems.

My Feed

5,996 cards cut from the app, 300–560 for every level, each with a reason, a question and a link to its exact topic — about twenty a visit, and then it ends.

Greek myths and authors

15 myths as a journey with the words they gave English; 14 author deep dives, Shakespeare deepest — every fact from the books and the Library’s own data.

Games with memory

Seven games remember what a child has met: new items first, missed ones back after two days — 84–100% new across ten plays. Every level is three rounds and a final, with stars.

The Stage

Every speaking level, plus the Elocution Contest against Bizzing Bee's rivals. The microphone measures time, pace, pauses and range on the device — nothing is recorded, and expression is judged by the child and a grown-up.

The writing desk

Seven kinds of writing, in parts, with a checklist the child ticks and counts — never marks. Only a grown-up's 1–4 rubric on a later day makes a piece “learned”. Nothing written leaves the device.

Practice

Spaced checks on a later day, and the mistakes deck. A stop is learned at 8 of 10 on a later day and mastered a week after that; a slip drops one step.
